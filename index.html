Lesson 12026-09-23

How every import duty is calculated — classification × valuation × origin

🎧 Trade Talk: How Every Import Duty Is Calculated

The idea

Every import duty is the product of three things. Classification (the HTS code) sets the tariff rate. Valuation sets the dutiable base — what the duty is charged on. Origin sets which rates apply and whether a free trade agreement can lower them. Get any one wrong and the duty paid is wrong.

Discussion question

For a piece of data-centre hardware, which of those three would be hardest to determine correctly, and why?

Proper answers (tap to reveal — try answering first)

Valuation — typically the hardest for tech hardware. Data-centre gear is usually bought from related parties (a foreign subsidiary or contract manufacturer), so the price may not be arm's-length and CBP scrutinizes related-party pricing closely. Assists must be added to the dutiable value: engineering, designs, tooling, or software the US company provides to the overseas factory. Embedded software/firmware and royalties can count too. Classification is a close second — multifunction hardware can straddle headings (e.g. 8471 computers vs 8517 communications equipment), and the essential-character test decides. Origin is the most mechanical of the three, though multi-country supply chains and marking rules still trip people up.

Lesson 22026-09-24

Reading a tariff code — HTS chapters, headings, subheadings

🎧 Trade Talk: Reading a Tariff Code

The idea

A 10-digit HTS code reads like a library call number, broad to specific. Digits 1–2 = chapter (99 chapters; chapter 85 is electrical machinery/electronics). Digits 3–4 = heading (a family of goods, e.g. 8471 = computers and units). Digits 5–6 = subheading — the international layer, shared by every country in the Harmonized System. Digits 7–10 = the US-specific breakdown that sets the actual duty rate. Errors at the chapter level are obvious; errors in the last two digits can sit quietly wrong for years until an audit finds them.

Why it matters

:** A program manager won't classify products herself — brokers do that — but she designs the process around classification: how product teams submit data to trade ops, how decisions get documented and revisited when products change, and how business teams learn that a "small design change" can move a product to a different code.

  2. A product has been imported under the wrong 10-digit HTS code for a year without CBP noticing. How does an error like this usually stay hidden, how does it typically surface, and what follows when it does?
  3. Engineering redesigns a product, swapping its main component. What two questions should trade compliance ask before the next shipment?
  4. A business team wants to "just use the code with the lowest duty rate" since the product could arguably fit two codes. What's wrong with that approach?
Proper answers (tap to reveal — try answering first)
  1. Your product's HTS code begins with 85. What does "85" tell you — and which digits of a 10-digit code actually determine the duty you pay?
    85 is the chapter. The first 6 digits are the international Harmonized System layer (identical in every member country); digits 7–10 are US-only and set the actual duty rate — so a code can be correct at 6 digits yet wrong and costly at 10.
  2. A product has been imported under the wrong 10-digit HTS code for a year without CBP noticing. How does an error like this usually stay hidden, how does it typically surface, and what follows when it does?
    The error hides because the product sits in the right category and clears customs without a flag. It usually surfaces through a CBP audit, an internal review before a big shipment, or a product change that triggers re-checking the code — the last being most common in tech. Underpaid duties plus penalties follow.
  3. Engineering redesigns a product, swapping its main component. What two questions should trade compliance ask before the next shipment?
    Ask (a) which component now gives the product its essential function — that's the essential-character test customs applies — and (b) whether trade ops was told before it shipped. The second is the process-design move: a trigger so engineering changes can't reach the border before compliance sees them.
  4. A business team wants to "just use the code with the lowest duty rate" since the product could arguably fit two codes. What's wrong with that approach?
    Importers have a legal duty of "reasonable care" — deliberately picking the lowest rate is the opposite of it. Penalties (19 U.S.C. § 1592) can run to several times the lost duties, so "close enough" is a compliance violation wearing a cost-saving costume.

Lesson 32026-09-25

The General Rules of Interpretation (GRIs) in practice

🎧 Trade Talk: The Tie-Breaker Rules

The idea

The HTS contains its own six tie-breaker rules, and they must be applied in order — 1, then 2, then 3, then 4, with 5 covering containers and packaging, and 6 repeating the logic one level down inside subheadings. GRI 1: classify by the heading's own wording plus the Section and Chapter Notes (the notes are law, not footnotes — read them first). GRI 2(a): an unassembled or incomplete article that already has the "essential character" of the finished good classifies as the finished good — so a server shipped as a kit of parts is still a server. GRI 2(b): a reference to a material covers mixtures and combinations of it. GRI 3 — the workhorse — applies when goods fit two or more headings: (a) the most specific description beats the general one; (b) mixtures, composite goods, and retail sets go by whichever component gives them their essential character (their main function or purpose); (c) if still tied, the heading that appears last numerically wins. GRI 4: goods that fit nowhere go with whatever they most resemble. GRI 5: a fitted case normally sold with its article (camera + fitted case) and ordinary packing materials classify with the article. GRI 6: apply rules 1–5 again, level by level, inside the subheadings.

Why it matters

:** This is a training and process-design topic. You won't rule on classifications yourself, but "fits two codes" is exactly where GRIs live — and where business teams most want to pick the cheaper rate. You'll train teams on that instinct, design classification-review templates that force brokers to show their reasoning trail ("GRI 1: heading terms…; GRI 3(b): essential character is…"), and recognize judgment-call territory that deserves a binding ruling instead of a guess.

Test yourself

  1. A product could fit two headings. What question does GRI 3(a) ask, and what question does GRI 3(b) ask?
  2. A server is imported as an unassembled kit and assembled at the destination. Which GRI decides its classification, and what is the result?
  3. A retail set containing a laptop, a fitted sleeve, and a mouse is sold together. How do the GRIs classify the set, and under which heading?
  4. Two importers face the same ambiguous classification. One documents its reasoning and seeks a binding ruling; the other picks the cheaper code because "the description fits." Which approach is compliant, and why?
Proper answers (tap to reveal — try answering first)
  1. A product could fit two headings. What question does GRI 3(a) ask, and what question does GRI 3(b) ask?
    3(a): "Which heading describes these goods most specifically?" — specific beats general. 3(b): "Which component gives this mixture/composite/retail set its essential character?" — its primary function or purpose decides.
  2. A server is imported as an unassembled kit and assembled at the destination. Which GRI decides its classification, and what is the result?
    GRI 2(a) — an unassembled article with the essential character of the finished good classifies as the finished good. The kit classifies as a server; the fact that assembly happens at the destination doesn't change it.
  3. A retail set containing a laptop, a fitted sleeve, and a mouse is sold together. How do the GRIs classify the set, and under which heading?
    GRI 1 puts each item in its own heading; GRI 3(b) then treats the retail set as one good — essential character is the laptop (it gives the set its purpose; the sleeve is a fitted case normally sold with it per GRI 5(a), and the mouse rides along as an accessory). The whole set classifies under the laptop heading.
  4. Two importers face the same ambiguous classification. One documents its reasoning and seeks a binding ruling; the other picks the cheaper code because "the description fits." Which approach is compliant, and why?
    A compliant process documents the reasoning: heading terms, Section/Chapter Notes, GRIs applied in order, and why the chosen code wins — reviewed by trade ops/the broker and kept on file. A genuine judgment call goes to CBP for a binding ruling (CROSS) instead of a guess. Picking the cheaper code because "the description fits" is the opposite of reasonable care.

Lesson 42026-09-26

Binding rulings (CROSS) — getting certainty on classification

🎧 Trade Talk: Binding Rulings, Certainty in Writing

The idea

Some products genuinely fit two codes, and no amount of internal reasoning settles it. For those, CBP lets you ask the question directly: a binding ruling is CBP's written answer, on your exact product, that CBP itself must follow. It's free, CBP has up to 90 days to respond (many rulings come back sooner), and it covers classification, valuation, origin, or marking. The request must describe your product in detail — samples, specs, photos — because the ruling only binds when the facts match exactly. Once issued, CBP is legally required to apply it to your imports as described, which locks in your duty costs and gives you an ironclad reasonable-care defense if CBP ever asks why you picked that code. Even better: every past ruling is searchable for free in CROSS (Customs Rulings Online Search System), so half the time someone has already asked your question. Two caveats: CBP won't rule on hypotheticals or goods already in court, and a ruling stays binding unless CBP revokes it — which requires publishing a proposed change in the Customs Bulletin and allowing public comment, so rulings are deliberately stable.

Why it matters

:** This is one of your best levers for enabling business. When trade ops and engineering argue about a classification — or a product launch is pricing against two possible duty rates — the ruling request is the program-manager answer: it converts a judgment call into a written, binding decision, and the 90-day window gets built into launch timelines. You'll also use CROSS the other way: teach teams that searching past rulings before importing a new SKU is cheap due diligence.

Test yourself

  1. You hold a binding CROSS ruling classifying your product. What exactly does it bind — and what happens if the product changes?
  2. A product launch is five months out and two classifications are debated internally. Should you request a ruling? Why?
  3. You find a 2019 CROSS ruling on a "very similar" product with a favorable classification. Can you rely on it? What should you check first?
  4. CBP wants to modify an existing ruling to your disadvantage. Can it just change it overnight?
Proper answers (tap to reveal — try answering first)
  1. You hold a binding CROSS ruling classifying your product. What exactly does it bind — and what happens if the product changes?
    The ruling binds CBP itself: once issued, CBP must apply that classification to your imports — but only for the transaction as described. If the product, materials, or facts change, the ruling no longer covers you. Reliance on a valid ruling is strong evidence of reasonable care (19 U.S.C. § 1592).
  2. A product launch is five months out and two classifications are debated internally. Should you request a ruling? Why?
    Yes — exactly the case rulings are for. Five months clears the 90-day response window comfortably; it locks in landed-cost pricing and ends the internal debate with a written CBP answer, so launch pricing and margin math stop being guesses.
  3. You find a 2019 CROSS ruling on a "very similar" product with a favorable classification. Can you rely on it? What should you check first?
    Not blindly. Check (a) the facts truly match your product — specs, components, function; "very similar" is not identical, and the ruling only binds for the transaction it describes; and (b) whether the ruling has been modified or revoked since 2019 (CBP publishes revocations in the Customs Bulletin). If facts match and it stands, it's persuasive and useful — but only a ruling on your product binds CBP for you.
  4. CBP wants to modify an existing ruling to your disadvantage. Can it just change it overnight?
    Not simply. CBP must publish the proposed modification/revocation in the Customs Bulletin and give interested parties 30 days to comment. That deliberateness is a feature: your ruling is stable, and you get warning before it changes.

Lesson 52026-09-27

Customs valuation I — transaction value

🎧 Trade Talk: What Your Imports Are Really Worth

The idea

An import duty is rate × value. Lessons 2–4 covered the rate side (classification); today the value side — because a perfect HTS code times the wrong value still pays the wrong duty.

CBP's preferred appraisal method is transaction value: the price actually paid or payable for the goods when sold for export to the US — the total payment the buyer makes to (or for the benefit of) the seller, directly or indirectly.

Statutory additions that must be included if not already in the price: (1) packing costs, (2) selling commissions paid by the buyer, (3) assists — engineering, designs, artwork, tooling, dies, molds, materials or components the buyer provides to the foreign producer free or at reduced cost, (4) royalties or license fees the buyer must pay as a condition of the sale, (5) proceeds of any later resale, disposal, or use of the goods that accrue to the seller.

One big exclusion: international freight and insurance to the US are not dutiable — America taxes the export-side value, unlike most countries that tax CIF value.

Five conditions must ALL be met, or transaction value is off the table: (1) a real sale for export to the US (leases, consignments, free transfers don't count); (2) no restrictions on how the buyer may use or resell the goods; (3) the price isn't subject to conditions whose value can't be determined; (4) no resale proceeds flow back to the seller (unless adjustable); (5) buyer and seller aren't related — or, if related, the relationship didn't influence the price (proven with a "circumstances of sale" test or test values).

When transaction value fails, CBP falls down a fixed ladder: identical merchandise value → similar merchandise value → deductive value (US resale price minus margins) → computed value (production cost plus profit) → last-resort reasonable means.

Why it matters

:** Enabling business with process-improvement muscle. Valuation drives landed-cost math on every product launch — and related-party pricing is one of the first things CBP audits. Valuation data is scattered across contracts, POs, invoices, and royalty agreements, so the program-design move is a standard intake worksheet: every new SKU forces the same question set — who made it, what did we provide to the factory, what royalties/commissions touch this sale? Capture it systematically at sourcing time, and trade ops stops discovering assists during an audit.

Test yourself

  1. What is the "price actually paid or payable," and name two statutory additions to transaction value. Name one cost excluded from dutiable value.
  2. Transaction value needs a sale for export to the US. Give two arrangements that aren't sales — and would knock an importer off transaction value.
  3. A US company imports servers from its wholly-owned Taiwan subsidiary at $900/unit; unrelated buyers pay $1,150 for identical units. The broker clears them at $900. What do you flag, and what do you ask trade ops to verify?
  4. A supplier offers to lower the invoice price and rebate the difference offshore, saying "everyone does it" — it would cut duty cost. How do you respond, and what's the real risk?
Proper answers (tap to reveal — try answering first)
  1. What is the "price actually paid or payable," and name two statutory additions to transaction value. Name one cost excluded from dutiable value.
    The price actually paid or payable is the total payment the buyer makes to (or for the benefit of) the seller for the goods sold for export to the US. Statutory additions include packing costs, assists (engineering, designs, tooling provided free or at reduced cost), selling commissions paid by the buyer, royalties/license fees paid as a condition of sale, and resale proceeds accruing to the seller. Excluded: international freight and insurance to the US — America taxes the export-side value, not CIF.
  2. Transaction value needs a sale for export to the US. Give two arrangements that aren't sales — and would knock an importer off transaction value.
    Leases and consignments aren't sales (no transfer of title for export); free transfers and samples don't count either. Related-party status alone does not disqualify — transaction value can still apply if the relationship didn't influence the price (that's condition 5, not a knockout). US goods returned to the US aren't a sale for export either, but they fall under separate duty-free provisions (9801.00.10), not the transaction-value ladder.
  3. A US company imports servers from its wholly-owned Taiwan subsidiary at $900/unit; unrelated buyers pay $1,150 for identical units. The broker clears them at $900. What do you flag, and what do you ask trade ops to verify?
    Flag the related-party price: $900 is only acceptable if the relationship didn't influence it — the $1,150 unrelated price suggests it may have. The broker clearing at $900 without that analysis is the compliance gap. Ask trade ops to run the circumstances-of-sale test or provide test values (e.g., computed value from manufacturing cost plus profit, or identical-goods sales to unrelated buyers).
  4. A supplier offers to lower the invoice price and rebate the difference offshore, saying "everyone does it" — it would cut duty cost. How do you respond, and what's the real risk?
    Decline, document, flag the supplier, and review prior transactions with them for the same scheme. The offshore rebate is part of the price actually paid or payable (an indirect payment), so declaring the lower invoice price is a false statement. Fraudulent violations under 19 U.S.C. § 1592 can draw penalties up to the domestic value of the goods, plus criminal exposure. "Everyone does it" is not a defense — reasonable care requires exactly the opposite.

Up next: Lesson 6 — Customs valuation II: assists, related-party pricing, and adjustments.

Lesson 62026-09-28

Customs valuation II — assists, related-party pricing, adjustments

🎧 Trade Talk: The Hidden Costs in Your Import Price

The idea

Yesterday's transaction value has three sharp edges worth their own lesson: assists, related-party pricing, and post-importation adjustments.

Assists are the value the buyer quietly contributes to production — and they must be added to the dutiable value. The statute lists four categories: (1) materials, components, or parts incorporated into the imported goods; (2) tools, dies, molds, and similar items used to produce them; (3) merchandise consumed in production; (4) engineering, development, artwork, design work, and plans undertaken outside the US and necessary for production. The valuation rule: use the cost of acquiring or producing the assist. The apportionment rule: spread it reasonably — usually over the production run it serves. Send $200,000 of tooling to a factory for an expected 100,000-unit run, and each unit carries $2 of assist value; a 10,000-unit entry declares $20,000.

Royalties and license fees are dutiable when paid as a condition of the sale of the imported goods. A royalty for the right to reproduce or manufacture in the US after importation is not dutiable. The test is what the payment buys: the imported goods themselves, or a separate post-importation right.

Related-party pricing got its deep dive in yesterday's Q&A: the $900-vs-$1,150 server case. Transaction value survives related parties only through Door A (circumstances of the sale — price set like the industry or like sales to unrelated buyers) or Door B (test values — the price closely approximates identical-goods sales, deductive value, or computed value). And the standing tension: the tax team's transfer-pricing study (IRS arm's-length standard) does not automatically satisfy CBP. Two regulators, two standards, one price.

Adjustments: when the real price isn't known at importation — the classic case is a year-end transfer-pricing true-up — CBP's Reconciliation Program lets importers flag entries as estimated and true up the declared value later, paying the additional duties with interest. Doing nothing and hoping is the alternative CBP audits for.

Why it matters

:** Assists are the purest process failure in trade compliance: engineering hands designs to a factory and nobody tells trade ops, so the value never enters the declaration. The program-design fix is the sourcing-time intake worksheet from Lesson 5, plus a standing related-party valuation file per product family, plus a standing meeting between tax and trade before year-end adjustments — so the reconciliation entry is planned, not panicked.

Test yourself

  1. Name the four statutory categories of assists.
  2. Your US team sends $200,000 of tooling to a factory in Malaysia for an expected 100,000-unit run over two years. The first entry covers 10,000 units. How much assist value is added to that entry, and what's the principle?
  3. You pay a 5% royalty to the brand owner as a condition of the sale of imported headphones. Dutiable? What if the royalty were instead for the right to manufacture the headphones in the US?
  4. Your tax team books a $2M upward transfer-pricing adjustment on goods already imported and cleared this year. What do you do?
Proper answers (tap to reveal — try answering first)
  1. Name the four statutory categories of assists.
    (a) Materials, components, or parts incorporated in the imported goods; (b) tools, dies, molds, and similar items used in production; (c) merchandise consumed in production; (d) engineering, development, artwork, design work, and plans undertaken outside the US and necessary for production.
  2. Your US team sends $200,000 of tooling to a factory in Malaysia for an expected 100,000-unit run over two years. The first entry covers 10,000 units. How much assist value is added to that entry, and what's the principle?
    $20,000 — $200,000 apportioned over 100,000 units = $2/unit × 10,000 units. Principle: apportion in a reasonable manner (typically over the production run served); if actual production falls short, recalculate per-unit value.
  3. You pay a 5% royalty to the brand owner as a condition of the sale of imported headphones. Dutiable? What if the royalty were instead for the right to manufacture the headphones in the US?
    The condition-of-sale royalty is dutiable — it must be added to transaction value. A royalty for the right to manufacture/reproduce in the US is a separate post-importation right and is not dutiable.
  4. Your tax team books a $2M upward transfer-pricing adjustment on goods already imported and cleared this year. What do you do?
    Use CBP's Reconciliation Program: the entries should have been flagged as estimated at importation; now true up the declared value and pay the additional duties with interest. Coordinate tax and trade prospectively so the next adjustment is anticipated, not discovered.

Up next: Lesson 7 — Country of origin and marking requirements.

Lesson 72026-09-29

Country of origin and marking requirements

🎧 Trade Talk: Where Your Goods Are Really From

The idea

Origin is the third leg of the Lesson 1 triangle — classification × valuation × origin. It decides which duty rates apply and whether a free trade agreement can lower them. For customs (nonpreferential) purposes, the legal test is substantial transformation: the country of origin is where the good was last processed into a new and different article of commerce — one with a different name, character, or use (19 CFR § 134.1(b)).

Worked example (steps labeled):

  1. The supply chain: PCBs fabricated in Taiwan, populated with Chinese-sourced components in Malaysia, firmware flashed and units tested in Malaysia, shipped from Malaysia.
  2. The test (the legal anchor): origin = the country of last substantial transformation — a change in name, character, or use (19 CFR § 134.1(b); tariff-shift from Lesson 3's GRIs is the practical screen).
  3. Apply it: bare board → populated assembly → flashed server unit is at least two transformations; the last one happened in Malaysia, so origin for marking = Malaysia.
  4. The marking rule (Section 304 of the Tariff Act, 19 U.S.C. § 1304): every article must be marked with its country of origin ("Made in Malaysia") — in a conspicuous place, as legibly, indelibly, and permanently as the article's nature permits, in English — and the marking must survive to the ultimate purchaser (the last person in the US to receive it in the form it was imported).
  5. The trap (the program-manager read): marking origin ≠ FTA-preference origin. A USMCA preference claim uses tariff-shift/RVC rules — a different test that can give a different answer for the same goods. She doesn't need to win origin disputes; she owns the process that keeps the two origins from being confused in the declaration pipeline.

Cost of getting it wrong: articles not legally marked are subject to an additional 10% marking duty on the appraised value (19 U.S.C. § 1304(f)), unless they're exported, destroyed, or properly marked under CBP supervision before liquidation.

Why it matters

:** Origin and marking decisions cross engineering, sourcing, packaging, and suppliers — classic cross-functional support work. Three questions for the team in week one: (1) how is country of origin determined for new products today, and does the answer distinguish marking origin from FTA-preference origin; (2) how is physical marking verified at import — supplier check, incoming QC, or nobody; (3) where does origin data live and who owns it. Listen for a documented, system-owned answer vs. a person-dependent one. The process move: vendor checklists that include marking verification, so a supplier's packaging decision can't create a marking-duty surprise.

Test yourself

  1. (Warm-up from Lesson 6) (a) Name the four statutory assist categories. (b) When is a royalty dutiable?
  2. State the substantial-transformation test for country of origin in one sentence.
  3. (a) Components from China and Taiwan are finally assembled into a server in Vietnam — what is the country of origin for marking purposes, and why? (b) The goods ship in plain brown boxes. Does that waive marking requirements?
  4. (Scenario) A colleague is confused: the marking analysis says Vietnam, but the FTA analysis says the goods don't qualify for preferential treatment. Is something wrong? What should be done?
Proper answers (tap to reveal — try answering first)
  1. (Warm-up from Lesson 6) (a) Name the four statutory assist categories. (b) When is a royalty dutiable?
    Warm-up (Lesson 6 retrieval): assists = (a) materials, components, parts incorporated in production; (b) tools, dies, molds and similar items; (c) merchandise consumed in production; (d) engineering, development, and design work outside the US — all at cost, apportioned over the production run they serve. A royalty is dutiable only if it's a condition of the sale of the imported goods (e.g. required to purchase them at all); a post-importation right (e.g. to reproduce or manufacture in the US) is not.
  2. State the substantial-transformation test for country of origin in one sentence.
    Substantial transformation — processing into a new and different article of commerce with a different name, character, or use. Origin = the country where the last such transformation happened. (A tariff shift at the heading level is the practical screen.)
  3. (a) Components from China and Taiwan are finally assembled into a server in Vietnam — what is the country of origin for marking purposes, and why? (b) The goods ship in plain brown boxes. Does that waive marking requirements?
    (a) Vietnam — final assembly into a server unit (a new and different article) is the last substantial transformation; Chinese/Taiwanese components don't change that. (b) Plain boxes don't waive marking: the marking must still be conspicuous, legible, indelible/permanent, in English — and must reach the ultimate purchaser. If the brown box is the retail container, mark the box; if units are pulled out of it before sale, mark the units themselves. (If US finishing/assembly were instead substantial transformation, the US importer becomes the ultimate purchaser and the marking exception of 19 CFR § 134.35 applies — but ordinary repackaging doesn't qualify.)
  4. (Scenario) A colleague is confused: the marking analysis says Vietnam, but the FTA analysis says the goods don't qualify for preferential treatment. Is something wrong? What should be done?
    Not automatically a problem. Marking origin (substantial transformation) and FTA preferential origin (tariff-shift/RVC rules) are different legal tests that can legitimately give different answers for the same goods. What to do: make sure both determinations are documented with their reasoning on file — and treat the confusion itself as a process signal, because mixing the two up in the declaration pipeline is exactly the error her role exists to design against.

Lesson 82026-09-30

Duty-savings programs: foreign trade zones (FTZs)

🎧 Trade Talk: The Zone That Isn't Quite the United States

The idea

An FTZ is a site inside the US that CBP treats as outside US customs territory. The four financial benefits: (1) duty deferral — no duty paid until goods leave the zone for US commerce (cash-flow win, unlimited storage time); (2) inverted tariff relief — if the parts carry a higher rate than the finished good, elect to pay the lower finished-good rate on withdrawal; (3) duty elimination on re-exports — goods exported from the zone never owed duty at all; (4) weekly entry savings — consolidate weekly withdrawals into one entry, paying one MPF instead of hundreds. Bonus: no duty on goods destroyed or scrapped in the zone. The price: FTZ Board authorization + CBP activation, and tight inventory-control/recordkeeping — sloppy records are the fastest way to lose the benefits (and get audited).

Why it matters

:** Compliance creates value — the inverted-tariff example (parts at 5%, finished goods at 2%) is the kind of number that gets a CFO's attention. The lane: enabling business by surfacing duty savings, cross-functional setup (operations, logistics, customs broker) to run the zone properly.

Test yourself

  1. Name three of the four financial benefits of using an FTZ, in your own words.
  2. A company imports sensor modules dutiable at 4% into its FTZ, assembles them into finished devices dutiable at 1.5%, and withdraws the devices into US commerce. What duty rate does it pay, and what is this benefit called?
  3. An importer receives 300 shipments a year and withdraws goods from its FTZ weekly. Explain in one or two sentences how weekly entry saves it money versus filing 300 separate entries.
  4. Defective units worth $200,000 are destroyed inside the FTZ instead of being imported. How much duty is owed on them, and why?
Proper answers (tap to reveal — try answering first)
  1. Name three of the four financial benefits of using an FTZ, in your own words.
    Any three of: duty deferral (pay only when goods enter US commerce), inverted tariff relief (elect the lower finished-good rate), duty elimination on re-exports, weekly entry savings (fewer entries, fewer MPFs).
  2. A company imports sensor modules dutiable at 4% into its FTZ, assembles them into finished devices dutiable at 1.5%, and withdraws the devices into US commerce. What duty rate does it pay, and what is this benefit called?
    1.5% — inverted tariff relief: the company elects the finished device's lower rate at withdrawal instead of the sensor modules' 4%.
  3. An importer receives 300 shipments a year and withdraws goods from its FTZ weekly. Explain in one or two sentences how weekly entry saves it money versus filing 300 separate entries.
    Instead of ~300 separate entries — each triggering its own MPF — the company files one weekly entry per withdrawal week (at most 52 a year), drastically cutting MPF and broker filing costs.
  4. Defective units worth $200,000 are destroyed inside the FTZ instead of being imported. How much duty is owed on them, and why?
    Zero duty owed — the units were destroyed or scrapped inside the zone and never withdrawn for consumption, so no duty attached; the destruction must be documented (typically under CBP supervision).

Lesson 92026-10-01

Duty-savings programs: drawback

🎧 Trade Talk: Getting Your Duties Back

The idea

If an FTZ is duty relief you set up before goods enter, drawback is the refund you claim after — up to 99% of the duties, taxes, and fees paid on imported goods that are subsequently exported (or destroyed) (19 U.S.C. § 1313; 19 CFR § 190.3). You pay at the border, then file to get it back.

Three main flavors: (1) Unused merchandise drawback — imported goods never used in the US, then exported (or destroyed under CBP supervision); export must happen within 5 years of import. (2) Manufacturing drawback — imported goods used to make something in the US that's then exported; the TFTEA modernization allows substitution, so you can claim on exported articles made from commercially interchangeable inputs at the same 8-digit HTS — no need to trace the exact physical units. (3) Rejected merchandise drawback — defective goods that don't meet specs, exported or destroyed.

Two things that separate this from a free money tree: not every duty qualifies — Section 301 duties are eligible, but Section 232 steel/aluminum duties and antidumping/countervailing duties are not (know exactly which duty you paid before you promise a refund). And the paper trail is the program: an unbroken chain from import entry → inventory/manufacturing records → export evidence, with claims filed electronically through ACE. Miss the documentation and there's nothing to claim; keep it and five years of past imports may still be recoverable — that's a lookback opportunity most companies leave on the table.

Why it matters

: Drawback is the purest enabling business story in import compliance — found money, measured in recovered duty dollars. The reason companies don't claim isn't legal; it's a process failure: purchasing, importing, inventory, and exporting data live in different systems with nobody linking them. A 30-day move: a lightweight import/export data-linkage pilot (match export docs to entry numbers) or a drawback-broker lookback analysis — and training** so teams flag export activity at import time, because you can't claim what you never tracked.

Trade remedy duties — Section 232, Section 301, and IEEPA tariffs

🎧 Trade Talk: The Extra Tariffs That Break Landed Cost

The idea

Lessons 1–7 were about the base duty — the normal MFN rate in the tariff schedule. Trade remedy duties are extra percentage duties stacked on top, imposed for policy reasons, not revenue. Three legal authorities generate nearly all of them:

  1. Section 232 (Trade Expansion Act of 1962): tariffs when imports threaten national security — the steel and aluminum tariffs are the famous ones, and investigations have covered semiconductors and other tech inputs.
  2. Section 301 (Trade Act of 1974): tariffs as retaliation for unfair foreign trade practices — the big one for tech: the China lists, with up to 25% extra on servers, networking gear, components, and thousands of other China-origin products.
  3. IEEPA (International Emergency Economic Powers Act): emergency-powers tariffs a president can impose quickly in a declared national emergency — the vehicle for the 2025 "reciprocal" tariff regime and country-specific emergency actions.

Four operational facts matter more than the acronyms:

  • They're calculated on the same dutiable (customs) value as the base duty. A 25% extra tariff on a $1,000 switch = $250 more, on top of whatever the HTS rate gives. Base rate × value still holds; remedies just add a second rate × same value.
  • They apply by country of origin — shift where something is made and the tariff may fall away… or a new one may attach.
  • Free trade agreements don't erase them. An FTA preferential rate replaces the base MFN rate, but remedy duties sit outside that — one of the most expensive misconceptions in import planning.
  • They can appear or change on short notice via executive order, proclamation, or Federal Register notice. Products with long ocean lead times can be mid-transit when the rate changes.

One pressure valve: exclusion processes (especially under Section 301) let importers request carve-outs for specific products. Granted exclusions are temporary and narrow — they help individual SKUs, but they're not a strategy.

Why it matters

:** This is enabling business and cross-functional support in one package. When a 25% tariff lands, product margins, pricing, and sourcing decisions all move at once — finance, sourcing, and supply chain need the same facts fast. The program-manager move is a standing process: who monitors new actions (trade ops), who re-runs landed-cost math (finance + pricing), who decides whether to shift origin or seek an exclusion (sourcing + trade compliance), and how the decision gets documented. You're also the trainer who kills two myths: "but we have an FTA" (FTAs don't touch remedy duties) and "we'll just do final assembly in another country" (origin is governed by substantial transformation — cosmetic assembly doesn't change it, and origin laundering draws enforcement).

The idea

What FTAs do — preferential duty rates in one picture

🎧 Trade Talk: The Zero-Duty Rate You Have to Earn

The idea

A free trade agreement is a deal between countries that boils down to one sentence: "if the goods genuinely come from one of us, charge little or no duty at the border." Each FTA creates a preferential rate — a second, lower rate that sits beside the normal MFN rate in the tariff schedule (the "Special" column, Lesson 2). The U.S. currently has 14 FTAs covering 20 countries — the big ones for tech supply chains being USMCA, Korea, Singapore, and Australia, plus Chile, Peru, Colombia, Panama, CAFTA-DR, Israel, Jordan, Morocco, Bahrain, and Oman.

The one-picture summary: for qualifying goods, the FTA rate replaces the base rate. A switch with a 5% base HTS rate, imported from a USMCA partner and properly claimed, enters at "Free" — $0 duty. But the preference is earned, not automatic, and it has exactly three conditions (each a full topic ahead):

  1. The goods must meet the FTA's rules of origin (Lessons 15–16) — where they were made, and from what inputs. Simply being shipped from a partner country doesn't make goods "originate" there.
  2. The importer must claim it at entry — by entering the FTA's Special Program Indicator (SPI) code, e.g. MX or CA for USMCA goods. No claim, no preference — the base MFN rate applies by default.
  3. It only replaces the base rate — trade remedy duties (Section 232/301, IEEPA — Lesson 10) sit outside FTAs and apply in full regardless of any preference.

Why it matters

:** FTA preferences are one of the cleanest ways a trade compliance program pays for itself — a "Free" rate can erase several percentage points of landed cost on qualifying goods, the kind of number supply-chain leadership hears loudest. But the preference is only as good as the evidence: origin documentation owned by sourcing and production teams, SPI codes entered correctly on the entry, and FTA claims that can survive a CBP origin verification years later. The program work here is process design, not memorization: mapping which SKUs are FTA-eligible, assigning ownership of the origin analysis, storing supplier certifications where they can be found, and training business teams on the two classic misconceptions — that the preference is automatic, and that FTAs erase trade remedy duties.

Origin rules — tariff shift and regional value content

🎧 Trade Talk: The Two Tests That Prove Your Goods Originate

The idea

Every FTA says "qualifying goods move duty-free" — but a rule decides who qualifies. Rules of origin exist to block a simple trick: routing non-party goods through a member country to dodge duties. There are two main tests a product can pass:

  1. Tariff shift. Each non-originating material must change its tariff classification during production — crossing a boundary the agreement names, such as a change of chapter (CC), change of heading (CTH), or change of subheading (CTSH). The logic: turning chapter-85 parts into a chapter-84 machine shows something real happened in the region, not just repackaging. Each FTA's annex lists the exact shift required per product heading.
  2. Regional value content (RVC). Where classification change is a poor test (complex electronics often keep the same heading from parts to finished good), the agreement allows math instead: prove enough of the value was created in the region. USMCA's two formulas are the build-up method (regional content ÷ transaction value, typically ≥60%) and the build-down method ((transaction value − non-originating materials) ÷ transaction value, typically ≥50%). Autos are special-cased much higher (75% RVC under net-cost method).

Two helpers complete the picture. De minimis forgives a small share of non-originating material that fails the tariff shift (USMCA: up to 10% of transaction value) — it cannot substitute for a failed RVC requirement. Accumulation (cumulation) lets originating inputs from one party count as fully originating when used in production in another party, so Mexican and Canadian inputs can be pooled.

Why it matters

:** An origin determination is a mini supply-chain investigation, not a form: sourcing supplies component origins, production confirms what processing happens where, finance supplies values for the RVC math, and trade operations files the claim. A trade compliance program turns that into a repeatable per-SKU workflow — ownership of the origin analysis, a certification routine, and five-year recordkeeping behind every claim — and trains business teams on the sentence that matters: "assembled in Mexico" is a fact about geography; "originates in Mexico" is a legal conclusion that must be proven.

FTA documentation and common mistakes

🎧 Trade Talk: The Paperwork Behind the Preference

The idea

A valid FTA preference claim is really two things bundled together: a correct legal conclusion (the goods meet the origin rules) and a provable one (you can show your work years later). The documentation side has three layers:

  1. The certification. For USMCA there is no prescribed form — any document signed by the importer, exporter, or producer works, as long as it carries the nine minimum data elements: who is certifying and in what role, the product description and its tariff classification, which origin criterion is claimed, the blanket period covered, and a signature with date. Other FTAs can be stricter about format, so the "any piece of paper" comfort is USMCA-specific. Certifications can be issued on a blanket basis covering up to 12 months of identical goods — convenient, and dangerous (see mistake 3 below).
  2. The evidence behind the certification. The certification is the cover page; the claim rests on the origin analysis: the bill of materials with each component's origin, supplier declarations or affidavits, production records showing what processing happened where, and the cost data behind any RVC math. CBP can demand this years after entry through questionnaires, record requests, or site visits — and if you can't produce it, the claim is denied.
  3. Five-year recordkeeping. Everything above must be retained for five years from the entry. An FTA program is, in part, a filing system with a five-year memory.

Then the five common mistakes — the ways companies keep losing these claims:

  1. "Shipped from Mexico" ≠ "originates in Mexico." Still the number-one misconception: transshipment and simple assembly don't confer origin; the rules of origin must be proven.
  2. Claiming on a supplier's word alone. A one-line "it's USMCA-qualifying" email is not an origin analysis. You need the supplier's supporting evidence — tier-2 sourcing is where claims silently die.
  3. Stale blanket certifications. A 12-month blanket certification doesn't update itself. A supplier switch, a BOM change, or new sourcing can invalidate the origin conclusion mid-year — and if nobody re-verifies, the company keeps claiming a preference it no longer qualifies for. That's when a documentation problem becomes a reasonable-care problem (Lesson 11).
  4. The forgotten or wrong SPI. No SPI code at entry means no preference, period — and the code must match the FTA actually claimed.
  5. Assuming the preference covers trade remedy duties. It never does (Lessons 10, 13, 14) — Sections 232/301 and IEEPA duties apply in full alongside any FTA preference.

Why it matters

:** FTA preference claims are one of the cleanest ways a trade compliance program pays for itself — a "Free" rate can erase several percentage points of landed cost — but only when the program's plumbing is sound. That plumbing is pure process design: mapping which SKUs carry FTA claims, assigning ownership of each origin analysis, building a change-notification routine so BOM and supplier changes trigger re-verification, storing certifications and evidence so they can be retrieved five years later, and training business teams to kill the five misconceptions. Done well, the documentation behind each claim converts directly into landed-cost savings the business can bank with confidence.

Other U.S. FTAs at a glance

🎧 Trade Talk: The Other U.S. Trade Deals

The idea

Beyond USMCA, the U.S. has 13 more FTAs in force — with Korea, Singapore, Australia, Chile, Peru, Colombia, Panama, Israel, Jordan, Morocco, Bahrain, Oman, and CAFTA-DR (Costa Rica, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, and the Dominican Republic). Three differences that trip people up:

  1. Origin-rule details differ. Tariff-shift annexes, RVC thresholds, and exceptions are agreement-specific. De minimis is around 10% in most agreements, but the exact base and carve-outs vary — the agreement text, not USMCA memory, governs. Cumulation rules also differ: who counts as a "party" and whether diagonal cumulation applies changes who you can pool inputs from.
  2. Certification requirements differ. USMCA is unusually relaxed — no prescribed form, and the importer, exporter, or producer may certify. Other FTAs can be stricter about who signs and what format or certificate is used, and blanket-certification periods vary (USMCA allows up to 12 months).

"Close trading partner" does not mean "preference available" — the only thing that matters is whether a specific agreement covers the goods and the claim is filed with the correct SPI.

One related note: beyond FTAs there are unilateral preference programs like the Generalized System of Preferences (GSP), which grant duty-free treatment to goods from designated developing countries. Unlike FTAs, these are discretionary government programs — they can lapse, be suspended, or be redesigned. Their rates are never as solid in a cost model as an FTA rate, and current status must be verified before banking the savings.

Why it matters

:** Sourcing teams will ask "which country gives us the best duty rate?" — the answer is never a guess from a list of FTAs; it is a per-SKU origin analysis run against the specific agreement. A trade compliance program answers this with one reusable workflow: a per-SKU origin analysis routine, a certification-and-recordkeeping process, and change-notification discipline — run against any FTA instead of rebuilt each time. That turns a scattered set of agreements into reliable landed-cost savings the business can model with confidence.

PGAs — who they are and why tech imports trigger them

🎧 Trade Talk: The Gatekeepers You Never Planned For

The idea

When a shipment arrives at a U.S. port, most people picture one gatekeeper: Customs and Border Protection. CBP collects the duties, checks valuation and origin, and releases the goods. But CBP is not the only gatekeeper — and for tech products, often not the strictest one. A Partner Government Agency (PGA) is any U.S. agency that controls whether specific kinds of goods may be admitted into the country. There are more than 40 of them. The ones that matter most for tech: the FCC (radio-frequency devices), the FDA (food, drugs, medical devices — and anything emitting radiation, like lasers or lidar), the CPSC (consumer product safety, including children's electronics), the EPA (energy efficiency standards, batteries and chemicals), the DOT (hazardous materials such as lithium batteries), and USDA/APHIS (wood packaging materials — even pallets).

The core distinction: CBP answers "how much duty is owed?" while PGAs answer "may these goods come in at all?" A shipment can sail through CBP's side and still be detained, refused, or seized on a PGA's behalf. In practice it works through ACE, the single electronic system brokers file entries in: the broker transmits a PGA "message set" — the data the agency requires — and if it is missing or wrong, the shipment gets flagged with a hold. CBP can even demand redelivery: hand back goods that were already released, because a PGA later said no.

Why does tech trigger PGAs so often? Because PGA rules are attribute-based, not tariff-based. An HTS code says the product is "a computer" — it doesn't say whether it has a Wi-Fi radio (→ FCC Part 15), a laser autofocus sensor (→ FDA radiation rules), a lithium battery (→ DOT hazmat), or children's appeal (→ CPSC). So the tariff code alone can't tell the broker which PGAs apply. Only the product's actual attributes can — and those are known long before the product ships, which means the compliance question has to move upstream to engineering and product teams.

Why it matters

:** PGA holds are the classic "compliance ambush": duty planning is done, the logistics timeline is set, and then a port hold appears that nobody modeled. A trade compliance program handles this through cross-functional support — the program manager builds the bridge between engineering (who knows the product attributes), product and supply chain teams (who know the launch timeline), and the broker (who files the message set). The program fix is a pre-shipment gate — a checklist asking "radio? laser? battery? consumer-facing? children's use?" — tied to the product release process, so PGA requirements are resolved before the freight leaves, not at the pier.

The idea

A shipment does not clear the border one agency at a time. CBP runs the gate, but FDA, FCC, CPSC, and the other partner government agencies issue their holds through the same port — CBP acts as their enforcement arm. A product can satisfy customs perfectly and still be examined, detained, refused, or destroyed at a PGA's request. So import compliance is not a stack of separate checklists; it is one gate with many locks, and the program has to coordinate every owner behind it. The coordination points that make this work: (1) one product-attribute intake — a standard questionnaire answered once per product ("RF-emitting? laser? battery? intended for children? food-contact?") that routes to each agency's rules; (2) document readiness before shipment — FCC grants/IDs, FDA registration numbers, CPSC certificates, test reports, gathered and transmitted through the ACE PGA message set before the goods arrive, not at the port; (3) a hold-response playbook — who answers a CBP hold issued at a PGA's request, in what time window, with what evidence; and (4) post-release follow-through — conditional-release conditions, recordkeeping, and fixing the root cause so the next shipment does not repeat the hold. The largest design failure in this space is timing: teams discover a PGA requirement at the port, when it needed to be in the launch timeline months earlier.

Why it matters

:** This is program-manager craft in pure form. A hold at the port is a business delay, a process failure, a training gap, and a cross-functional breakdown all at once. One shared intake and one pre-shipment gate replace ad hoc emails between engineering, suppliers, brokers, and trade ops — fewer surprises, predictable clearance, and launch teams that learn to trigger compliance early instead of being blindsided.

The EAR framework and the ten General Prohibitions

🎧 Trade Talk: The Ten Rules That Govern Every Export

The idea

The Export Administration Regulations (EAR), administered by the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS), are the rulebook for most commercial exports from the United States — and, through reexport rules, for some exports from other countries too. An item is "subject to the EAR" if it is U.S.-origin, located in the United States, a foreign-made item containing more than a de minimis amount of U.S.-origin controlled content, or a foreign-made direct product of U.S. technology or software. At the heart of the EAR sit the ten General Prohibitions (Part 736): ten things that may not be done without a license where one is required. In short: (1) exporting or reexporting controlled items to destinations other than Canada when a license is required; (2) reexporting or transferring in-country foreign-made items with controlled U.S.-origin content above the de minimis threshold; (3) reexporting foreign-produced direct products of U.S. technology or software; (4) acting contrary to a BIS denial order; (5) exporting to prohibited end-users or end-uses (Part 744); (6) exporting to embargoed destinations (Part 746); (7) U.S. persons supporting proliferation activities; (8) in-transit shipments and unladen exports through the United States; (9) violating the terms of a license or license exception; and (10) proceeding with a transaction knowing a violation has occurred or is about to occur. Nearly every export question a program manager faces can be traced back to one of these ten.

Why it matters

:** The General Prohibitions are the spine of an export compliance program: each one maps to a control a program can build — classification, destination screening, end-use checks, denial-order screening, embargo controls, and a "stop and escalate" rule for anything known to be wrong. They also explain why export controls reach beyond U.S. borders: prohibitions 2 and 3 follow U.S. content and technology into foreign-made products, which is exactly why a global company's supply chain cannot treat export control as a U.S.-only concern.

ECCN classification — reading a code, order of review, EAR99

🎧 Trade Talk: The Five Characters That Decide an Export

The idea

Lesson 22 set out the ten General Prohibitions; classification is the step that decides whether any of them bite for a particular item. An ECCN (Export Control Classification Number) is a five-character code from the Commerce Control List (CCL, EAR Part 774) that says what an item is, in export-control terms. It reads left to right: the first digit is the category (0 = nuclear, 1 = materials and chemicals, 2 = materials processing, 3 = electronics, 4 = computers, 5 = telecommunications and information security, 6 = sensors and lasers, 7 = navigation and avionics, 8 = marine, 9 = aerospace and propulsion). The letter is the product group (A = systems, equipment and components; B = test, inspection and production equipment; C = materials; D = software; E = technology). The last three digits are the specific entry, and sub-paragraphs (for example .a, .b) narrow it further by technical parameters — speed, capacity, encryption strength, and the like. So 5A002 means: category 5 (information security, part 2 of category 5), group A (a system or component), entry 002 (information security systems, equipment and components).

The practical order of review for classifying an item is: (1) Jurisdiction first — if the item is a defence article on the U.S. Munitions List, it falls under ITAR, not the EAR; (2) Is it subject to the EAR? — using the Lesson 22 tests (U.S.-origin, in the United States, foreign-made with controlled U.S. content above de minimis, or a foreign-made direct product of U.S. technology); (3) Search the CCL by what the item does and its technical parameters, not by its product name — the list is organised by function, and a name search misses entries; (4) if an entry describes the item, that entry is its ECCN; if no entry describes it, the item is EAR99. Companies generally self-classify and document the reasoning; where the answer is genuinely uncertain, BIS will classify the item on request. A classification is only as good as its inputs: change the chip, the software, or the performance, and the classification must be re-checked.

EAR99 is not "uncontrolled." It means only that no CCL entry describes the item. EAR99 items are still subject to the EAR, and the General Prohibitions still apply — a licence may still be required, or a shipment may still be prohibited, because of the destination (for example an embargoed country), the end-use, or the end-user (Part 744 restricted parties). Most EAR99 shipments to ordinary commercial destinations go under "NLR" (no licence required), but EAR99 is a classification result, not a permission slip.

Why it matters

:** Classification is the first gate in every export workflow: the ECCN, read together with the destination, determines whether a licence is needed at all (the subject of the next lesson). Getting it wrong poisons everything downstream — a wrong ECCN produces a wrong licence determination, and an EAR99 label applied by guesswork hides a controlled item. The program work is therefore process design: an intake that forces engineering to supply the technical parameters classification actually turns on, a written record of the reasoning for each classified item, a re-check trigger when the design changes, and training that kills the "EAR99 means ship anywhere" myth before a sales team acts on it.

The Commerce Country Chart and licensing policy

🎧 Trade Talk: The Chart That Turns a Code Into a Shipping Answer

The idea

Lesson 23 ended with a classification — an ECCN. This lesson turns that ECCN into a shipping answer: does this item need a licence for this destination? The tool for that is the Commerce Country Chart (EAR Part 738), and it works in three steps.

Step 1 — Find the item's reasons for control. Every ECCN entry in the Commerce Control List has a "License Requirements" section listing its reasons for control, each with a two-letter code: NS (national security), MT (missile technology), NP (nuclear nonproliferation), CB (chemical and biological weapons), RS (regional stability), CC (crime control), AT (anti-terrorism), EI (encryption items), and a few others. A single item can carry several reasons — for example, an information-security product classified as 5A002 is typically controlled for NS and EI.

Step 2 — Read the chart. The Country Chart is a grid: reasons for control run down the rows, destination countries across the columns. Find the row for each reason and the column for the destination. An X in that cell means a licence is required for that reason to that destination. If any reason produces an X, a licence is required (unless a licence exception applies — step 3's companion). If no reason produces an X, the shipment is NLR (no licence required) for that destination. Two things to note: Canada's column is nearly empty — most items ship to Canada NLR — and the chart answers only "is a licence required?", never "will it be granted?"

Step 3 — Check licensing policy. Once a licence is required, EAR Part 742 (plus the policy notes in the ECCN entry) states how BIS will generally treat the application: presumption of approval, case-by-case review, or presumption of denial. The policy depends on the reason and the destination together — for instance, a national-security item to an ordinary commercial destination is typically reviewed case by case, while an item controlled for missile-technology reasons to a destination of proliferation concern faces a presumption of denial. Policy is a forecast, not a promise: BIS still decides each application on its facts, and a presumption of denial means the application needs a strong justification, not that applying is pointless.

Two cautions complete the picture. First, a licence exception (Part 740) — such as ENC for encryption items, or TMP for tools of trade — can authorise a shipment even where the chart shows an X, but only if every condition of that exception is met; exceptions are permissions with fine print, not loopholes. Second, the chart covers only the item-and-destination question. The end-use and end-user rules (Part 744, next lesson) apply on top of it: an NLR shipment to an ordinary destination can still be blocked if the end-user is restricted or the end-use is prohibited.

Why it matters

:** The Country Chart is the decision point where classification becomes a business answer — ship, apply, or stop. A program turns this three-step read into a repeatable workflow: classification hands over the reasons for control, a destination check against the chart produces the licence determination, and the licensing policy sets expectations for timelines and the strength of the application. Building that check into the order or shipment workflow — rather than leaving it as a manual lookup done under deadline pressure — is what keeps licence determinations consistent across teams and destinations, and it gives sales and logistics a clear, early answer instead of a last-minute surprise.

Restricted end-uses and end-users (Part 744); BIS restricted lists

🎧 Trade Talk: Who Gets It and What They Will Do With It

The idea

Lessons 22–24 built the item-and-destination answer: classify the item, read the Commerce Country Chart, check licensing policy. Part 744 sits on top of that answer. It controls shipments based on who receives the item and what they will do with it — and it applies even when the chart says NLR and even when the item is EAR99.

Part 744 has two halves. Restricted end-uses: certain uses are controlled no matter what the item is. The core set is weapons-of-mass-destruction-related: nuclear end-uses (744.2), missile-related end-uses (744.3), and chemical- and biological-weapons-related end-uses (744.4). Alongside those sit military end-use and military end-user controls (744.21) for a listed set of destinations, including China and Russia: items destined for a military end-use or a military end-user there can require a licence even if the same item to a commercial customer would not. Restricted end-users: BIS maintains lists of named parties that trigger a licence requirement or a ban. The four to know are the Entity List (named entities subject to licence requirements spelled out per entry, generally under a presumption of denial — and the requirement applies even to EAR99 items), the Denied Persons List (persons and companies whose export privileges have been revoked, who generally may not participate in an export transaction at all), the Unverified List (UVL) (parties whose bona fides BIS could not verify — shipments to them lose licence-exception eligibility and require a UVL statement from the party), and the Military End User (MEU) List (entities identified as military end-users, triggering licence requirements for certain items to listed destinations).

The glue holding all of this together is the knowledge standard: under the EAR, "knowledge" includes not only actual knowledge but also reason to know. BIS's "Know Your Customer" guidance lists red flags — an evasive or newly formed customer, a vague or shifting stated end-use, an unusual routing request through a third country, payment in cash or from an unrelated party, an order inconsistent with the customer's line of business. A red flag does not prove a violation, but it must not be ignored: the correct response is to inquire, document the answers, and escalate to trade compliance if the concern is not resolved. Deliberately looking away — self-blinding — counts as knowledge. Note the boundary: these BIS lists are export-control lists, distinct from OFAC's sanctions lists (such as the SDN List), which are covered in later lessons; a party can appear on one system and not the other, so screening must check both.

Why it matters

:** Part 744 is where export control stops being a classification exercise and becomes a transaction-level gate. Classification and the Country Chart answer the item-and-destination question, but only end-use and end-user screening catches the deal that looks clean on paper and is not: the EAR99 shipment to a listed entity, or the ordinary component headed for a military programme. A programme therefore needs screening built into the order and shipment workflow — not as a one-off check at the end — plus training that teaches customer-facing teams to recognise red flags and escalate rather than rationalise them away. That design is classic programme-manager work: one gate, clear ownership, and an escalation path that sales teams actually use.
