Lesson T12026-10-30

The trade tech stack, mapped

The idea

No single system "does trade compliance." A stack of layers does, and each layer has one job. (1) Systems of record — the ERP (orders, shipments, invoices) and the PLM (product designs, bills of material) hold the raw facts: what the product is, who is buying it, where it is going. (2) Global trade management (GTM) platforms — products such as SAP GTS, Oracle GTM, Thomson Reuters ONESOURCE Global Trade, Descartes, and e2open — hold the determinations: classification, license decisions, FTA qualification, duty calculation. (3) Screening engines check every party on a transaction — customer, supplier, consignee, bank, carrier — against government restricted-party lists. (4) Regulatory content services keep the raw material current: tariff schedules, ECCN lists, sanctions lists, and rule changes, which update constantly. (5) Filing connections — EDI or API links to brokers and to customs systems — move the approved data out as declarations. (6) Document and analytics layers store the proof and surface the metrics.

Follow one export order through the stack: the ERP creates the order; the screening engine checks the parties; the GTM platform confirms the product's ECCN and determines whether a license is needed for that destination and end-user; documents are generated from the same data; the filing layer transmits; the document layer archives everything for the five-year recordkeeping clock.

The classic failure is layer confusion: expecting the ERP to screen (it has no lists), expecting the broker's portal to classify (it files what it is given — garbage in, garbage filed), or running the whole program on a spreadsheet (no audit trail, no change control, one person's memory as the system of record). A program manager's first tech job is simply to draw this map for her own company and mark which layer does what — and which layers are missing.

Build vs. buy is a layer question too. Companies rarely build content (the lists and schedules change too fast — that is what content subscriptions are for), sometimes configure a GTM platform, and often discover their real gap is integration: systems that don't talk to each other force humans to re-key data, and every re-keying is an error opportunity.

Why it matters

:** Every later technology decision — automating, adding AI, choosing a vendor — is really a placement decision: which layer does this belong to, and what feeds it? Teams that can draw their stack make those decisions in days. Teams that can't, buy tools that duplicate what they have, miss what they lack, and find out during an audit.

Test yourself

  1. A shipment is held because a consignee matches a restricted-party list — but the order was created three days earlier. Which layer failed, and when should it have acted?
  2. Your customs broker offers to "handle classification" through their portal at no extra cost. What is the layer confusion in that offer, and what risk stays with your company?
  3. Why do companies almost never build their own regulatory content, even when they build other software in-house?
  4. A company runs its trade program on spreadsheets maintained by one expert. Name two distinct risks in that setup, tied to two different layers of the stack.
Proper answers (tap to reveal — try answering first)
  1. A shipment is held because a consignee matches a restricted-party list — but the order was created three days earlier. Which layer failed, and when should it have acted?
    The screening layer. Party screening belongs at order creation (and again at shipment, because lists and parties change), not at the dock. A hold discovered at shipping means screening ran late or not at all in the flow — the fix is moving the screening engine's check upstream into the order process in the ERP/GTM flow.
  2. Your customs broker offers to "handle classification" through their portal at no extra cost. What is the layer confusion in that offer, and what risk stays with your company?
    A broker portal is a filing layer: it transmits the classification it is given. Classification is a determination, and the legal duty of reasonable care for it stays with the importer/exporter of record no matter who keys it in. Accepting the offer without a determination process behind it means unreviewed codes get filed — and the liability is still yours.
  3. Why do companies almost never build their own regulatory content, even when they build other software in-house?
    Because content is a moving target, not a build-once asset: tariff schedules, ECCNs, and sanctions lists change constantly, and a missed update is a compliance failure. Content services spread the cost of tracking every regulator across thousands of customers; a single company building it in-house pays full price for the same vigilance, forever.
  4. A company runs its trade program on spreadsheets maintained by one expert. Name two distinct risks in that setup, tied to two different layers of the stack.
    Any two of: no determination audit trail (the spreadsheet shows a result, not who decided it, when, or from what data — a recordkeeping failure); no content update mechanism (codes and lists go stale silently); single-person dependency (the system of record is one employee's memory and habits); no integration (every hand-off is manual re-keying, so filing data drifts from source data).

Up next: Lesson T2 — Automation that actually works: what to automate first, and where automation turns small errors into big ones.

Lesson T22026-10-30

Automation that actually works

The idea

Automation is excellent at trade tasks that are structured and repeatable: screening parties against lists, determining whether a license is required from an ECCN + destination + end-user, checking FTA qualification against origin data, generating standard documents, preparing entry data for the broker. Given clean inputs, a rule engine makes the same correct decision ten thousand times without fatigue — that's the win: consistency, speed, and an automatic audit trail.

Automation is dangerous exactly where its inputs are ambiguous. A vague product description ("module, electronic") automated into a classification workflow doesn't produce a thoughtful decision — it produces a confident wrong answer at scale. Trade professionals call this the blast radius problem: a manual error affects one shipment; an automated error affects every shipment until someone notices. An automated FTA claim built on one wrong origin field can run for a year and turn into a six-figure repayment plus a prior disclosure.

So the professional skill isn't "automate everything." It's designing the boundary between machine and human:

  • Straight-through processing for the clean majority: complete data, low risk, established products and parties — no human touch, full logging.
  • Exception queues for everything else: incomplete data, new products, controlled items, screening near-matches. Every exception has an owner, a service-level target, and an escalation path — an exception queue nobody works is just a delayed violation.
  • Override logging: when a human overrules the system, the reason is recorded. Overrides are the system's best teacher — a pattern of overrides means the rule or the data is wrong, not the humans.
  • Change control for rules: determination logic and content updates are tested before release (including edge cases), because a bad rule change is itself an automation incident.

And you prove it's working with four metrics: straight-through rate (too low = no real automation; suspiciously perfect = exceptions are being waved through), exception rate and aging, cycle time from order to clearance-ready, and the post-entry amendment rate — the ground truth of whether the automation's decisions were right.

Why it matters

:** Business teams ask trade compliance for two things that sound contradictory: faster and safer. Well-bounded automation is how a program delivers both — and how a small team covers a large company's transaction volume. Poorly bounded automation is how a small error becomes a systemic one. The difference is entirely in the design choices above, and designing them is program work, not IT work.

Proper answers (tap to reveal — try answering first)
  1. Which of these would you automate first, and which would you keep human-reviewed longest: (a) screening repeat customers against updated lists, (b) classifying a brand-new product line, (c) generating export documents from approved data, (d) deciding whether a novel end-use falls under Part 744 controls?
    Automate first: (a) and (c) — both are structured, repeatable, and driven by data that is either externally maintained (the lists) or already approved (the document data). Keep human-reviewed longest: (b) and (d) — new-product classification and novel end-use analysis are judgment calls on ambiguous inputs; automate the routing and data collection around them, not the decision.
  2. An automated FTA qualification check has been running for 14 months. An audit finds the origin field it relied on was wrong for one product family. Why is this worse than the same mistake made manually, and what single control would have caught it in the first month?
    Blast radius: the manual mistake affects the shipments one person touched; the automated rule applied the wrong field to every shipment of that family for 14 months — the repayment, interest, and disclosure scope multiply accordingly. The control that catches it early is sampling: a small, scheduled human re-check of automated determinations (say, a monthly sample per product family), which exists precisely to detect silent rule/data failures while they are still small.
  3. Your straight-through rate is 99.8% and the exception queue is nearly empty. Leadership is delighted. Why aren't you?
    Because a near-perfect straight-through rate usually means the exception logic is broken, not that trade became simple: thresholds too loose, data problems being defaulted instead of flagged, or staff clearing exceptions without review to keep the queue empty. Healthy automation shows a visible, worked exception stream. Verify by auditing a sample of "straight-through" transactions by hand.
  4. A business team keeps overriding the system's license determinations "to keep shipments moving." What are two different problems this could signal, and what's your first move for each?
    Problem one: the system is wrong — its rules or data misfire on legitimate business, and overrides are the symptom; first move is to review the override log for a pattern and fix the rule/data. Problem two: the culture is wrong — determinations are correct but treated as optional under shipping pressure; first move is escalation and governance: overrides of license determinations get restricted, logged, and reviewed, because an undocumented override of a license decision is exactly what enforcement actions are made of.

Up next: Lesson T3 — AI for classification and screening: what AI can genuinely do, and the guardrails that make it defensible.

Lesson T32026-10-30

AI for classification and screening

The idea

AI — including large-language-model agents — is genuinely good at a specific slice of trade work: reading messy product descriptions and datasheets, drafting an HTS or ECCN classification with reasoning, finding the rulings and chapter notes that bear on it, summarizing a regulation change, and triaging screening results (most name matches are false positives, and sorting the obvious ones is tireless, repetitive work). Used this way, AI is a fast first drafter and a tireless first filter.

What AI cannot be is accountable. The importer's duty of reasonable care and the exporter's license liability belong to the company and its people, and "the model said so" is not a defense — worse, an LLM can produce a plausible, well-formatted, completely wrong code with total confidence (a hallucination), or reason from outdated rules it absorbed in training. An unaudited AI classification doesn't stay wrong once; like any automation, it gets reused on every future shipment of that product.

The workable model is tiered human-in-the-loop:

  • AI proposes; it must show its work — the product attributes it relied on, the headings/entries it considered, the rulings or notes it cites (retrieved from authoritative content, not recalled from training), and a confidence level with reasons.
  • Review depth scales with risk: low-duty, well-understood consumer goods with high model confidence may need only spot review; controlled items (real ECCNs, encryption, sensors), high duty impact, or new product types always get full expert review before use.
  • Before go-live, the system is benchmarked: run it against a "golden dataset" of products whose classifications experts have already settled, and measure agreement. You would never let a new broker file untested; an AI agent gets the same probation.
  • Everything is logged: inputs, sources, output, confidence, reviewer, decision. That audit trail is the reasonable-care evidence — it converts "AI did it" into "here is the determination process, and here is the human who owns it."

Two more guardrails matter in practice: confidentiality (unreleased product specs are company crown jewels — which AI tools may see them, and where does that data go?) and drift (models and content change; a system validated in January needs re-validation when either updates).

Why it matters

:** Classification is high-volume, expert-scarce work — exactly where AI leverage is largest, and exactly where an error compounds. Teams that learn to benchmark, tier, and audit AI output get real speed with defensible decisions. Teams that paste AI answers into filings get a future enforcement case study. For a trade program manager, AI fluency is quickly becoming the job: designing these guardrails is program work.

Test yourself

  1. An AI agent proposes an HTS code with "92% confidence" for a new product. List three things you check before that code is used, and one piece of evidence you save either way.
  2. Why does grounding the AI in retrieved rulings and tariff text matter more than using a "smarter" model that answers from its training?
  3. Your pilot shows the AI agrees with expert classifiers on 96% of the golden dataset. The 4% disagreements cluster on dual-use electronics. What do you do with that result — ship it, shelve it, or something smarter?
  4. A year after go-live, an auditor asks how a particular AI-assisted classification was decided. Walk through what you hand her, step by step.
Proper answers (tap to reveal — try answering first)
  1. An AI agent proposes an HTS code with "92% confidence" for a new product. List three things you check before that code is used, and one piece of evidence you save either way.
    Check: (a) the product attributes the AI relied on against the actual spec — AI reasoning on wrong inputs is worthless however elegant; (b) the cited sources — open the rulings/notes and confirm they say what the AI claims and actually govern this product; (c) the alternatives it rejected and why, especially any heading with a very different duty or control consequence. Save: the full determination record — inputs, sources cited, confidence, your review decision and name — in the classification file.
  2. Why does grounding the AI in retrieved rulings and tariff text matter more than using a "smarter" model that answers from its training?
    Because trade law is specific, current, and citable. A model answering from training may blend rules across countries, use superseded text, or invent a ruling that sounds right. Retrieval grounding forces the answer to stand on documents you can open, verify, and show an auditor — the AI becomes a research and drafting tool over authoritative content, not an oracle.
  3. Your pilot shows the AI agrees with expert classifiers on 96% of the golden dataset. The 4% disagreements cluster on dual-use electronics. What do you do with that result — ship it, shelve it, or something smarter?
    Something smarter: tiered deployment. Launch AI-assisted classification for the categories where agreement is high, and route dual-use electronics — the cluster where it's weakest and the consequences of error are highest (export controls, not just duty) — to mandatory full expert review, with the AI limited to drafting and research. Then feed the expert decisions on those hard cases back into the golden dataset and re-benchmark.
  4. A year after go-live, an auditor asks how a particular AI-assisted classification was decided. Walk through what you hand her, step by step.
    You hand her the audit trail: the product data submitted (description, specs, BOM) as it stood that day; the AI's proposal with the rulings, notes, and schedule text it retrieved and cited; its confidence and reasoning; the review record — who reviewed it, what they checked or changed, and the final determination; and the benchmark/validation status of the AI system version in use at the time. Together that shows a process of reasonable care, with a named accountable human — which is what she is actually auditing.

Up next: Lesson T4 — Data as a control: why the product master decides whether every system above tells the truth.

Lesson T42026-10-30

Data as a control

The idea

Every determination in the tech stack — classification, screening, license, FTA claim, duty — is computed from data, mostly the product master: the company's record for each item. The trade-critical fields are a short list: accurate product description, HTS code, ECCN, country of origin, value components (price, assists, royalties), end-use notes, license information, supplier and factory identity, and the bill of materials (BOM) behind origin and classification calls. If those fields are right, even modest systems perform well. If they're wrong, the most expensive GTM platform in the world just automates falsehoods with excellent uptime.

So treat data quality as what it legally is: a control. In audit after audit, the root cause is rarely a misread regulation — it's a field nobody owned. An engineer swaps a key component (classification and ECCN may change) and no trigger tells trade. A factory quietly adds a second plant in another country (origin changes; FTA claims and remedy-duty exposure change with it) and procurement updates the PO but not the master. A supplier changes its legal name and screening silently stops matching.

Data-as-a-control means four practices:

  • Ownership: every trade-critical field has a named owner — not "the system," a person or team accountable for its accuracy.
  • Edit discipline: who may change an ECCN or an origin, and what review that change requires, is restricted and logged — these fields are compliance decisions, not clerical entries.
  • Change triggers: engineering change orders, new suppliers, factory moves, and price-structure changes automatically open a compliance review task. The trigger is the control; hoping people remember to email trade is not.
  • Refresh cycles: supplier and origin data expire. Certificates are re-collected, screening data (names, aliases, addresses) is kept matchable, and dormant products are re-validated before they ship again.

Screening data deserves a special note: matching quality is a data problem on both sides — the government's list entries and your party master. Clean, complete party data (full legal names, addresses, known aliases) is what separates a screening program that catches real matches from one drowning in false positives.

Why it matters

:** This is the unglamorous lesson underneath all the others: automation (T2) scales whatever the data says, and AI (T3) reasons over whatever the data holds. A program manager who fixes ownership and triggers in the product master has improved every downstream system at once — often more than a new software purchase would.

  1. A contract manufacturer moves production of your top-selling item from Malaysia to Vietnam mid-year. Which product-master fields may now be wrong, and name two trade consequences that follow if nobody updates them.
  2. Who should be able to edit a product's ECCN in the master — anyone on the trade team, the item's data owner with review, or the engineer who designed it? Defend your answer in one or two sentences.
  3. Engineering releases a change order swapping a product's main processor for a more capable one. List the trade reviews that change should trigger before the next shipment.
  4. Your screening tool reports a 98% false-positive rate. Before blaming the tool, what two data-quality checks do you run on your own party data?
Proper answers (tap to reveal — try answering first)
  1. A contract manufacturer moves production of your top-selling item from Malaysia to Vietnam mid-year. Which product-master fields may now be wrong, and name two trade consequences that follow if nobody updates them.
    Fields: country of origin (and factory/supplier identity), plus anything origin-dependent — FTA qualification status and any origin-based duty treatment. Consequences (any two): FTA claims filed on Malaysian origin become invalid for Vietnamese goods (repayment + interest when caught); remedy or standard duty rates applied are the wrong country's; marking ("Made in") on the product and paperwork is now false; screening/embargo exposure changes if the new country carries different restrictions.
  2. Who should be able to edit a product's ECCN in the master — anyone on the trade team, the item's data owner with review, or the engineer who designed it? Defend your answer in one or two sentences.
    The item's data owner, with compliance review — because an ECCN is a legal determination, not a spec. The designing engineer is an essential source (she knows what the item does) but shouldn't unilaterally set the control classification; and open editing by a whole team destroys accountability for who decided what, when.
  3. Engineering releases a change order swapping a product's main processor for a more capable one. List the trade reviews that change should trigger before the next shipment.
    Re-classification review (essential character and function may have moved the HTS heading), ECCN re-determination (a more capable processor can move an item into a controlled entry — e.g., performance thresholds), license impact for existing destinations, origin/RVC recalculation if sourcing value shifted, and updates to any filings, datasheets, or classification records that cite the old configuration — all before the next shipment, which is why the trigger must be automatic.
  4. Your screening tool reports a 98% false-positive rate. Before blaming the tool, what two data-quality checks do you run on your own party data?
    Check completeness and format: are party records carrying full legal names (not abbreviations, nicknames, or truncated ERP fields), and are addresses/aliases present and current? Fragmented or sloppy party data forces the matcher to guess broadly, which manufactures false positives. Second: duplicates and stale records — the same party entered five ways gets matched five ways, inflating the hit pile.

Up next: Lesson T5 — Dashboards and continuous monitoring: catching exceptions while they can still be fixed.

Lesson T52026-10-30

Dashboards and continuous monitoring

The idea

Traditional compliance testing is periodic: once a year, someone samples last year's transactions and finds last year's problems — after the goods have moved, the duties were paid (or not), and the disclosure clock has been running. Continuous monitoring flips that: the systems watch live operations and surface exceptions while they can still be fixed — a shipment about to go to a newly listed party, a license about to expire with orders still queued against it, a classification backlog growing faster than the team clears it.

A useful trade dashboard watches a handful of things, grouped by the question they answer:

  • Are we about to break a rule? Screening hits awaiting disposition (and their age), shipments flagged for license review, licenses near expiry or near their quantity/value limits.
  • Is the machine working? Straight-through rate, exception rate and aging by queue, override counts, content-update status (are the lists and schedules current?).
  • What is it costing us? Duty spend by lane and rate type, FTA utilization (how much eligible trade actually claimed preference — money left on the table is a metric too), broker filing accuracy, post-entry amendment rate.

The key distinction is leading vs. lagging indicators. Penalties, amendments, and audit findings are lagging — they record damage. Backlogs, aging exceptions, expiring licenses, and falling FTA utilization are leading — they predict damage while it's still optional. A mature dashboard is built mostly of leading indicators, with lagging ones kept as the scoreboard.

Two failure modes to design against: alert fatigue (thresholds so loose the dashboard cries wolf until everyone ignores it — then it misses the wolf) and vanity metrics (big comforting numbers — transactions screened! — that measure volume, not risk). Every alert needs an owner, a threshold with a reason, and a defined action; otherwise it isn't a control, it's decoration.

All of it rolls up to one page for management: current risk exceptions, volumes, deadlines, and trends. That page is what governance actually runs on — it's how a trade team shows leadership the program's state in five minutes, and how problems get resources before they become disclosures.

Why it matters

:** Findings your own monitoring catches are process improvements — cheap, quiet, fixable. The same findings caught by CBP or BIS are audits, penalties, and voluntary disclosures. Monitoring is what moves a program from "we believe we're compliant" to "we can show you, this week, where we're not — and what we're doing about it."

  2. Your dashboard shows 400 open exceptions across all queues. Give two readings of that number — one alarming, one healthy — and one follow-up question that tells you which is true.
  3. FTA utilization is 61%: of the imports that could claim a preference, only 61% did. Is that a compliance problem, a business problem, or both? What do you do with the number?
  4. Design the test: an alert fires whenever a screening hit is undispositioned for 24 hours. Six months in, the team has muted it. What most likely went wrong, and how do you fix the alert rather than the team?
Proper answers (tap to reveal — try answering first)
  1. Sort these into leading or lagging indicators: (a) penalty assessed by CBP, (b) screening exceptions older than 48 hours, (c) post-entry amendment rate, (d) export licenses expiring within 30 days with open orders.
    Leading: (b) and (d) — both describe risk accumulating before a violation occurs, with time left to act. Lagging: (a) and (c) — both measure outcomes after the fact. (The amendment rate has a foot in both camps: it reports past errors, but a rising trend is an early warning about current process health.)
  2. Your dashboard shows 400 open exceptions across all queues. Give two readings of that number — one alarming, one healthy — and one follow-up question that tells you which is true.
    Alarming reading: controls are generating exceptions faster than owners clear them — risk is queuing up undispositioned. Healthy reading: the system is sensitive and catching borderline cases in a high-volume week, with owners working the queue to SLA. Follow-up: what is the aging profile — if the median exception is a few hours old and the oldest is within SLA, it's health; if a tail is weeks old, it's a backlog becoming a violation pipeline.
  3. FTA utilization is 61%: of the imports that could claim a preference, only 61% did. Is that a compliance problem, a business problem, or both? What do you do with the number?
    Both, wearing one hat. As a business matter, 39% of eligible preference unclaimed is duty overpaid — real money. As a compliance matter, low utilization is a diagnostic: it usually means origin data, supplier certificates, or qualification checks are too weak or too slow to claim confidently — the same weaknesses that produce invalid claims when people do claim. The follow-up work (fix data and documentation so claiming is safe) helps both sides at once.
  4. Design the test: an alert fires whenever a screening hit is undispositioned for 24 hours. Six months in, the team has muted it. What most likely went wrong, and how do you fix the alert rather than the team?
    Most likely the threshold ignored workload reality: routine false positives take longer than 24 hours to clear in normal queues, so the alert fired constantly on non-emergencies until it trained everyone to ignore it — classic alert fatigue. Fix the alert: tier it (24 hours for high-risk matches — controlled goods, listed-party near-matches; longer, or digest-form, for routine triage), and alert on aging trend and SLA breach rather than a single blunt clock. An alert that only fires when action is genuinely needed gets acted on.

Up next: Lesson T6 — Selecting and landing a trade tech solution: turning everything in T1–T5 into a purchase and a rollout that actually works.

Lesson T62026-10-30

Selecting and landing a trade tech solution

The idea

Trade technology projects rarely fail on features. They fail on fit (the tool solved a problem the company didn't quite have) and on adoption (the business kept using spreadsheets around it). The sequence that works runs in a specific order — and the software selection is step three, not step one.

Step 1 — Define the problem in business terms. Not "we need a GTM platform," but: screening takes two days per order and delays shipments; classification backlog is six weeks; we overpaid an estimated amount in unclaimed FTA preference; an audit finding says our license checks are manual. Each problem gets a measurable stake: risk reduced, cost avoided, cycle time cut.

Step 2 — Decide the approach. Build (rarely right for content-heavy trade functions), buy a platform, extend what you already own (many ERPs have trade modules nobody switched on), or improve the process first — some "software problems" are actually the data and trigger problems from Lesson T4, and no purchase fixes those.

Step 3 — Evaluate vendors on what matters in trade. Content coverage and update cadence (which countries, lists, and schedules; how fast do changes land?). Auditability (can it show who/what decided, from which data and content version?). Integration effort with your ERP and broker flow. Security and data handling (it will hold your product and customer data). And the demo rule: vendors demo on their sample data beautifully — insist on a proof of concept using your real, messy products, parties, and edge cases. The messy cases are the purchase.

Step 4 — Count the whole cost. License plus content subscriptions (recurring, and they grow), implementation, integration, data cleansing, training, and the internal owner who runs it forever. Compare against the whole benefit from Step 1, not against zero.

Step 5 — Implement in phases and land it. Cleanse the data first (T4), pilot with one product line or lane, test edge cases deliberately (the controlled item, the sanctioned-party near-match, the FTA edge case), train the business users whose workflow actually changes, and run hypercare after go-live. Adoption is designed, not hoped for: the tool has to live inside the order and shipping flow, because a compliance tool people must remember to visit is a compliance tool people forget to visit.

Why it matters

:** This lesson is where the whole track cashes out, and it's the program-manager skill set in miniature: translating compliance needs into a business case a finance partner can approve, evaluating technology with a regulator's eye for evidence, and landing change in other teams' daily work. Get this right and technology stops being a purchase and becomes what it should be — the program, running at the speed of the business.

Proper answers (tap to reveal — try answering first)
  1. A vendor's demo is flawless: every classification instant, every screen elegant. Write the three proof-of-concept demands you make before signing, and why each one matters.
    Any three of: (a) run your real product and party data, including known hard cases, through the system — demo data hides content gaps and integration pain; (b) show the audit trail for a determination — inputs, content version, rule path, user actions — because auditability is what you'll be judged on later; (c) demonstrate a content update — pick a recent list or schedule change and show when it landed and how it propagated; (d) test the integration with your actual ERP/order flow, not a file mock-up; (e) produce references in your industry and volume range. Prove it by baselining utilization and duty paid on eligible lanes before go-live, then reporting the same measures quarterly from the system itself — the claim was a number, so the proof is the same number, moved.
  3. Mid-implementation, testing reveals your product master is missing origin data for 30% of items. The vendor suggests going live anyway and "cleaning as we go." What do you say, and what's the phased alternative?
    You say no — going live with 30% missing origin data means 30% of determinations are guesses wearing a system's authority, which is worse than honest manual work. The phased alternative: launch with the product families whose data is complete (where the tool can genuinely run), and run the data-cleansing workstream for the rest as a gated phase two, with those families staying on the existing manual process until their data passes the same completeness bar.
  4. Go-live succeeded technically, but three months later the sales ops team is exporting orders to a spreadsheet and screening "the old way." Name the step in this lesson that failed, and the design principle that prevents it.
    Step 5 — landing/adoption — failed; technically the go-live was Step 3's success mistaken for the project's. The preventing principle: design the tool into the business workflow (screening inside the order flow, holds that only the system can release) rather than alongside it, and involve the using teams from Step 1 so the workflow is theirs. If the compliant path isn't the easiest path, people route around it.
